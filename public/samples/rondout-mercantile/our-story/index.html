Our story

A river port shop, since 1912

Four generations, one family, and the same few things done carefully: cloth by the yard, clothes made to measure, and good things for the house.

Founded by Augustin Delorme

A shop that started at the water

In 1912, Augustin Delorme opened a dry goods store a short walk from the Rondout docks. Bolts of linen and wool came up the Hudson by boat from New York, most of them shipped from France. He sold them by the yard to people who had their clothes made, and kept a bench in the back for a tailor.

The shelves filled out over the years. Children’s clothes. Soaps and scents from England. Spices, herbs and remedies behind a long apothecary counter. Honey from beekeepers up the valley.

Today the family still runs the shop. We cut cloth on the original counter, make clothes to measure in the back room, and keep the shelves to things we would use ourselves.

Timeline

From the docks to West Strand

Cloth came by boat from New York, most of it from France. Soap came from England. Honey came down from the hills. Some of that has not changed at all.

  1. 1912

    A dry goods store on West Strand

    Augustin Delorme rents a narrow brick storefront near the Rondout docks. Linen, wool and cotton arrive by river boat, most of it from France, with locally woven cloth beside it.

  2. 1920s

    A tailor’s bench in the back

    Customers buy cloth at the front and are measured at the back. A suit, a dress or a christening gown, made for one person.

  3. 1930s

    Soaps from England, spices behind the counter

    Shower soaps and scents arrive from England. The apothecary counter grows: dried herbs, spices and simple remedies, weighed to order.

  4. 1960s

    The neighborhood changes. The shop stays.

    Much of the old Rondout is torn down around us. The family keeps the doors open and the counter where it was.

  5. 2019

    The fourth generation

    We restore the storefront, reopen the tailoring workroom, and bring back cloth from France alongside cloth from the valley.
